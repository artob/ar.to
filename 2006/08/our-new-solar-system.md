Unless you’re living under a rock, you’ve probably already heard of this. The
[International Astronomical
Union](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Astronomical_Union),
aka the Committee on What to Call Astronomical Stuff, has today
[adopted](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://www.iau2006.org/mirror/www.iau.org/iau0603/)
an official definition of what is — and isn’t — a planet, in the process
stripping
[Pluto](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto)
of the title it has held since its discovery in 1930. Pluto has now been
reclassified as a [dwarf
planet](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_planet),
meaning our solar system has 8 proper planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars,
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

[![Our New Solar
System](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/artob/ar.to/master/src/content/essays/2006/08/20060824-solarsystem-new.jpg)](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://www.iau2006.org/mirror/www.iau.org/iau0603/iau0603a.html)

*Our new [solar
system](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_system).*

Text books and encyclopedias will need to be revised accordingly, of course. Not
surprisingly,
[Wikipedia](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/) has
already locked down its [solar
system](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_system)
and
[planet](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet)
pages, among others, and is in the process of updating their contents.

Personally, I think the IAU’s resolution was spot on. Pluto has for decades
seemed the oddball of the solar system. With the recent discoveries of further
trans-Neptunian bodies (namely [2003
UB<sub>313</sub>](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_UB313),
popularly known as Xena), and the high likelihood of discovering thousands more
similarly-sized [Kuiper
belt](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuiper_belt)
bodies in the future, an official definition of the term “planet” was overdue.

While dropping Pluto’s planet status has been described as a triumph of science
over sentiment, the [Bad
Astronomer](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://www.badastronomy.com/)
rightly [points
out](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://www.badastronomy.com/bablog/2006/08/24/breaking-news-pluto-not-a-planet/)
that defining what constitutes a planet is not a scientific decision but rather
an arbitrary cultural classification. Nonetheless, the IAU’s new guidelines read
lucidly enough:

- A **planet** is a celestial body that is in orbit around the Sun, has
  sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it
  assumes a [hydrostatic
  equilibrium](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic_equilibrium)
  (nearly round) shape, and has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit.
- A **dwarf planet** is a celestial body that is in orbit around the Sun, has
  sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it
  assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape, has not cleared the
  neighborhood around its orbit, and is not a satellite.

Pluto got the boot specifically due to the provision that a planet must clear
its neighborhood; Pluto fails this requirement since its eccentric and oblong
orbit overlaps with Neptune’s — in fact at
[perihelion](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelion)
of Pluto’s 248-year orbital period bringing it closer to the Sun than Neptune
for a couple of decades at a time (most recently, from 1979 to 1999). Of course,
its case wasn’t helped by the fact that it really **is** quite tiny (heck, it’s
smaller than the Moon!)

IAU’s new resolution certainly seems a bit smarter than their [harebrained first
proposal](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://www.badastronomy.com/bablog/2006/08/15/congratulations-its-a-planet/),
announced last week, which would have promoted Xena,
[Ceres](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_Ceres)
(the largest asteroid) and
[Charon](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charon_%28moon%29)
(Pluto’s moon) to planet status — giving us a 12-planet solar system, with the
number promising to keep ever increasing as more Pluto-like icy bodies are
discovered in the Kuiper belt. Now, Xena, Ceres and Charon are all considered
“dwarf planets”.

The new planet definitions only apply to our own solar system. So far,
[extrasolar
planets](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrasolar_planet),
of which [over 200 have been
discovered](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stars_with_confirmed_extrasolar_planets),
have no classification scheme, though similar criteria will no doubt be applied.
More than likely there are enough surprises awaiting in other solar systems to
keep the committee arguing semantics for the next couple of centuries.

### Putting the Solar System into Perspective

Incidentally, check out these magnificient 3D-rendered pictures of the main
solar system bodies. Showing the planets and the Sun in their correct relative
scale, the pictures give some idea of just how tiny Earth is in comparison to
the gas giants and the Sun.

Looking at the above picture, it may not be altogether inconceivable that three
Earths could actually fit inside the [Great Red
Spot](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Red_Spot),
the gargantuan storm that has raged on Jupiter for at least the past three
centuries.

While this picture demonstrates the majestic scale of the Sun compared to even
our largest gas giants, the Sun is actually not very large as far as stars go.
For instance, the nearby (in astronomic terms) red supergiant
[Betelgeuse](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betelgeuse)
is more than **40 million** times more voluminous than the Sun — if it were
placed in the location of the Sun, Betelgeuse’s
[photosphere](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosphere)
would extend beyond the orbit of Mars. To picture this, imagine that if
Betelgeuse were represented by the Sun in the above picture, then the Sun itself
would be the size of the tiny, almost invisible Earth.

### Most of the Universe is Missing — No, Really

In other science news this week,
[NASA](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://www.nasa.gov/) has
[found](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://chandra.harvard.edu/photo/2006/1e0657/)
the first direct evidence that [dark matter really
exists](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://cosmicvariance.com/2006/08/21/dark-matter-exists/).
Until now, [dark
matter](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter)
was a mere theoretical construct necessary to explain (among other things) how
[galaxy
clusters](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_cluster)
hold together, and there was always the possibility of dark matter being an
[illusion](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://www.newscientistspace.com/article/dn8631.html)
rising from some flaw in our understanding of gravity. However, the new
observations of the collision of two galaxy clusters, some 3 billion
[light-years](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_year)
away, strongly suggest that dark matter is very real, though still very much of
a
[mystery](https://web.archive.org/web/20060824/http://video.google.com/videoplay?docid=-8114580690921328857).
